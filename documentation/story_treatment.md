## What This Is

This document picks up after the current build's stopping point: Monday evening,
with the first quiz behind him and the weekend introductions made. Everything
from the prologue through that Monday is built — the airport, the first day, the
first free afternoon, the first river run, the second morning, the haiku
lecture, the Hamada Denki phone-plan errand, the maid cafe, Hikari's room visit,
the Saturday library and Lupin's Den introductions, the Sunday practice quiz,
and the Monday quiz. What follows is a detailed narrative treatment of the
remaining story, covering the full arc of every confidant, the escalation of the
simulation mystery, the Tokyo locations the protagonist visits, and the three
endings the game can arrive at.

It is written to inspire, not to be copied. The dialogue is suggested tone, not
final script. Take what works and rewrite the rest.

Dates in this treatment follow `documentation/calendar.md`. The built story
ends on Monday, April 13 (Day 6); the continuation begins on Tuesday,
April 14 (Day 7).

## Act 2: The Long Middle

The scripted portion of Act 2 runs from the first morning in the house through
the Monday of the first quiz. On the morning after the first free afternoon —
the second morning — the protagonist wakes in his room, Hikari walks him toward
school, the professor lectures on haiku, and Hikari takes him to Hamada Denki in
Akihabara to sort out a cellular plan. That afternoon he explores the district
alone and ends up inside the maid cafe, which is where Aya's arc starts. The
script then runs through the first weekend and Monday's quiz. The hub-driven
afternoons this treatment describes pick up after that Monday.

This is the act that holds everybody else's stories rather than the
protagonist's own. He is the lens through which five people grow, change, and
leave. The simulation does not care which order he visits them in, and neither
does the story. Every confidant arc runs independently, capped by act
progression so a player cannot finish one person's entire story before the
world has moved enough to let the next chapter of it mean something.

The hub opens on the first free afternoon after the current build's Monday —
the day-loop scene that calls it is not written yet (see `plan.md`). The phone
already has WIRE contacts on it — Hikari, the chef, Sim, and Aya by the end of
day two, with Hana and Orson added by the Saturday introductions — and the hub
adds the list of places: the five confidant locations plus the two walks home.

### How New Characters Enter

The game introduces its confidants the way the opening days introduce the chef,
Sim, Aya, Hana, and Orson: through the natural geography of a student life.
Nobody is announced. Nobody has a tutorial card. The protagonist walks into a
place, and the person is already there, being themselves.

**Orson** is found the way every student in Tokyo finds a bar eventually: by
walking the wrong street at night. The protagonist gets lost a second time, or
takes a detour after a late lecture, and finds a narrow door with a cloth over
it and a small wolf painted on the sign. The bar is called Lupin's Den. Inside, a
man in a wolf mask polishes a glass and says nothing until the protagonist sits
down. The introduction is the silence.

**Hana** is found through an errand, not by wandering. Exams are coming — the
clerk's attendance rules have been circling since orientation — and the
protagonist needs a quiet place to study and a department document he cannot
find anywhere else. Hikari, who knows the building better than he does, tells
him the library is where students go when they actually need to get something
done: some people are there to read, some to study, and they share the same
peaceful rooms. He takes the corridor she describes, finds a small room off it
with four chairs and a shelf of returns, and there is a girl shelving books who
will not look at him. A regular is there too, reading quietly, and explains in
one line who Hana is without Hana having to say a word.

**The Fortune Teller** is found the way fortune tellers are always found in Tokyo:
at a festival stall, or on the steps of a temple, or on a street corner at
night. She appears once per chapter, in a location the protagonist is already
visiting for another reason. She never seeks him out. He simply turns a corner
and there she is, with her cards on a folding table and her smile that knows too
much.

### The Rhythm of Days

Each day in Act 2 follows the same shape with different content:

1. **Morning** at the house. Breakfast with the family. Tetsuo gives a rule.
Miharu worries. Hikari is already leaving. The first hub day (Day 7) adds the
midterm briefing to this step; later hub days keep the same shape.
2. **Lecture** at the university. The professor teaches, the protagonist
listens, and the lecture topic always carries a thematic echo of what is
happening in the confidant arcs.
3. **Free afternoon** via the hub. One visit per day. The protagonist picks
where to go, and the game takes him there. The list holds the five confidant
places plus the two walks home. Day 7 is the first day this step is real: the
hub opens after the lecture, and the evening carries the picked place home
through `last_afternoon_place`.
4. **Evening** at the house. Dinner at eight. The family talks. The protagonist
carries what happened that afternoon home with him, and sometimes the house
notices.
5. **Night** in his room. The phone buzzes. WIRE messages arrive. The dream
comes, and it is getting worse.

Between the days, the simulation leaks. Hospital flashes come more often. The
glitch in the dream gets harder. Tetsuo repeats a sentence he said last week.
Miharu pauses in the middle of pouring tea. Hikari asks a question she has
already asked, and this time she hears herself do it.

## The Confidant Arcs

Every confidant has seven visits. After the seventh, they are gone. The place
they lived in is still there, but the person who made it matter is not. That
pattern is the thesis of the game: getting close to someone is what makes them
leave. Chef and Hana are the two documented exceptions — their stories run one
visit longer, to the closed shop and the closed reading room, which is the end
of the arc rather than a departure.

### The Ramen Chef — Seven Bowls

The chef never speaks English. Hikari translates on visits 4 and 6. Every other
visit is a conversation conducted in gestures, food, and the few Japanese
words the protagonist slowly accumulates. The arc is about earning a place at a
counter where nobody invited him, and then watching the counter empty.

**Visit 1: The Wrong Bowl** *(already scripted)*
He points blindly, gets something he did not order, and it is the best thing he
has eaten in Japan. The chef watches him eat without comment, then says one
word at the bowl: umai. If the protagonist asks what it means, the chef says it
again, slower, and points at the bowl until he repeats it back. That is the
lesson — the first word of the language he owns, taught mid-bite by the man
who never speaks it.

**Visit 2: Recognition**
The chef looks up when the cloth door moves. That is the greeting. He puts down
a bowl without being asked, and it is the same bowl as last time. Then he says
one word slowly, pointing at it: oishii. The protagonist repeats it badly. The
chef says it again, slower. The protagonist tries again. The chef nods once and
goes back to the pots. That is the lesson.

**Visit 3: The Spicy Misfire**
The protagonist tries to order something new. He points at a different line. The
chef looks at him, looks at the line, and makes it anyway. It is karai. The
protagonist eyes water. The chef watches this happen, says karai, and puts a
glass of water in front of him without being asked. Then he laughs. It is the
first time the protagonist has heard him laugh, and it is a short, rough sound
that dies immediately. The miscommunication has become part of the charm.

**Visit 4: Hikari at the Counter**
The protagonist brings Hikari. She orders for both of them in Japanese that moves
too fast for him to follow, and the chef responds in sentences instead of grunts
for the first time. Through Hikari, he learns the recipe is his grandmother. The
shop is fifteen years old. He came to Tokyo from the countryside and never went
back. Hikari translates a joke about the photograph on the wall that the chef
has never explained before. The protagonist is starting to feel like part of
the local rhythm, not just a visitor.

**Visit 5: The Usual**
The protagonist sits down and the bowl arrives without being ordered. It is the
right one. The chef gestures at the photograph on the wall for the first time
without being asked about it. A countryside shop. A woman in an apron. The
protagonist does not need Hikari to understand what that photograph means. The
chef puts a small paper coaster beside the bowl. It has the shop name printed on
it. It is the closest thing to a gift he has ever given a customer.

**Visit 6: The Weight**
Hikari translates again, and this time the words are heavier. The chef parents
are old. They need him. He does not know who takes over the shop. He has been
making the same bowl for fifteen years and he does not know who he is if he
stops. The protagonist watches a man measure the end of something he built his
whole life around. The chef does not ask for advice. He just needs someone to
hear it.

**Visit 7: Omoide**
The shop is too quiet. No steam. No radio. The chef makes one last bowl with the
care of a ceremony. He serves it, waits for the protagonist to finish, and puts
a small object on the counter. A wooden ramen spoon, hand-polished, the shop
name carved into the handle. He says one word: omoide. Memory. Then he turns
back to the pots, which is the conversation over. The next time the protagonist
walks down that street, the shutter is down and the cloth is gone.

### Sim — Seven Frames

Sim arc is about the room, not just the man. The club fills up, the photographs
get better, and the protagonist goes from observer to someone whose work is on
the wall. Then Sim gets the call he has been waiting for his whole life, and the
room he built dissolves the moment he succeeds.

**Visit 1: The Door with the Tape** *(already scripted)*
He arrives with a phone and no camera. Sim seats him in the circle, has him
shoot with the phone, and gives him the only subject that matters: the light on
the wall beside the window. He takes a photograph or two. Neither looks like
the ones on the wall, and Sim says exactly that — a little crooked, perhaps,
but the club can work with it. That is the Sim rule from the first hour: what
he says about the work is always smaller than the truth.

**Visit 2: The Name and the Basics**
Sim remembers his name. That is the whole first minute. Then he shows him how to
hold the phone like a camera, how to frame a doorway, and how to wait for the
light to change before pressing the button. He switches between Japanese and
English without noticing, and the room follows him in both languages. The
protagonist first photograph that is not an accident comes out here.

**Visit 3: The Good Shot**
The protagonist takes a photograph that is genuinely good. He knows it is good
because the room goes quiet for a second when Sim holds it up. Sim tells him to
keep it, and then tells him about the years of magazine submissions that came
back with silence. He says it like a fact, not a complaint. That is the Sim rule:
what he says about himself is always smaller than the truth.

**Visit 4: The Room Fills Up**
More people come. Sim manages them all without seeming to manage anything. A
woman named Rika brings prints and argues about composition. The protagonist is
no longer the newcomer; someone else is, and Sim introduces him as one of us.
The group takes a photo together, and it is the first time the protagonist has
been in a photograph since he arrived in Japan.

**Visit 5: The Quiet Night**
Few people come. The room is almost empty. Sim sits on the table instead of a
chair, which he never does when the room is full. He tells the protagonist that
before Circle he had no friends. No network. Only submissions that went
unanswered. He built the group because he needed a reason to be in a room with
people. He looks at the wall of prints and says: this group feels real because
you are here too.

**Visit 6: The Editor Email**
Sim shows the protagonist an email on his phone. A magazine editor saw his work
through the Circle group feed. They want to meet. They are in Osaka. It is real
recognition, the kind he spent years chasing, and it means leaving Tokyo. He
promises an exhibition first, and the room buzzes with it. He looks quietly
conflicted, and the protagonist understands why before Sim says it.

**Visit 7: The Exhibition**
The club room becomes a gallery for one night. Prints on every wall. Members
bring friends. The room is loud and proud. In the middle of it, Sim announces he
is leaving. He gives the protagonist a camera strap identical to his own. He
says someday we will walk with our cameras again. The group takes one last
photograph together. The next week, the room is still there, the club still
meets, but the person who made it glad to be somewhere is gone.

### Orson — Seven Masks

Orson is found, not introduced. The protagonist takes a wrong turn after a late
evening lecture, or walks a street he has not walked before, and finds a narrow
door with a cloth over it. A small wolf is painted on the sign. The bar is called
Lupin's Den. Inside, a man in a wolf mask polishes a glass and says nothing.

The mask never changes expression. That is the point: it is a wall, and he
knows it. The bar is the one place in Tokyo where the protagonist speaks English
with an adult who is not Hikari, and the conversations run long because Orson
never rushes anyone.

**Visit 1: The Silence**
The protagonist sits down at the counter. Orson puts a glass of water in front of
him without being asked and says nothing — or almost nothing. Two lines is all
the night gives him: "You found us. Most people don't," and, on the way out,
"The water's on the house. The conversation isn't. But you're welcome to it."
Cracked but not explained. The bar is narrow, dim, and warm. Three other
customers sit along the counter, all in masks, none of them talking to each
other. There is a small wooden bowl by the till for notes to the Master — he
does not use it, but he notices it exists, and that is the hook that brings him
back.

**Visit 2: The Mask Explained**
Orson initiates. He asks where the protagonist is from, and listens to the answer
the way a person listens to weather. Then he explains the masks. The rule of
Lupin's Den is that everyone wears one, and the reason is not secrecy. With a mask,
you can be anyone. Even yourself, if you are lucky. He says it lightly, but the
weight is there. The protagonist notices that the mask makes Orson easier to
talk to, not harder, and does not understand why yet.

**Visit 3: Bar Stories**
Orson tells stories about the people who come through. A businessman who comes
every Friday in a fox mask and cries into his whisky. A university professor
who wears a plain white face and reads the same newspaper for three hours. A
tourist who found the bar by accident and comes back every trip to Japan. The
stories are funny and warm and the protagonist starts to understand that the bar
is a sanctuary for people who need to be seen without being recognised.

**Visit 4: The Past**
Orson hints that he has not always run a bar. The protagonist asks and Orson
does not dodge the question; he just makes it smaller. Ventures that did not
work. Family expectations that were never met. The mask gave him the freedom to
build something of his own. He asks the protagonist what he thinks of Japan so
far, and the protagonist says something honest, and Orson nods like he expected
nothing else.

**Visit 5: The Advice**
Orson notices the protagonist seems more comfortable and teases him about it.
Then he says the thing that lands: it is okay to feel lost. Just do not stop
walking. He says it the way he says everything, lightly, without weight, but the
protagonist carries it home. This is the visit where the bartender becomes a
confidant.

**Visit 6: The Refuge**
Orson is more open than he has ever been. He talks about failed ventures, the
mask as both freedom and hiding place, and the bar as the one thing he built that
worked. He tells the protagonist that he reminds him of himself when he first
came to Tokyo: uncertain, curious, and too stubborn to leave. The protagonist
asks if he would go back and do it differently. Orson says he would not change
the bar. He would change arriving alone.

**Visit 7: The Mask Comes Off**
Orson tells the protagonist the bar is closing. Rising rent, a neighbourhood
moving on, a till that no longer balances. He does not pretend it is fixable. He
thanks the protagonist for coming as often as he did. Then he reaches up and
takes the mask off. It is the first and only time. His face is ordinary. Tired.
Kind. He gives the protagonist a worn wolf-paw badge, small enough to fit in a
pocket. The next time the protagonist walks down that street, Lupin's Den is a
generic coffee shop, bright and anonymous, full of strangers whose faces mean
nothing.

### Aya — Seven Smiles

Aya arc is about the gap between the performance and the person. She is the maid
who calls him Master, draws cats in ketchup, and chants moe moe kyun with a
straight face. She is also the woman who taught herself English because she
wanted to go further than the cafe door, and who stops wearing the costume the
moment she decides to leave.

**Visit 1: The First Spell** *(already scripted — it plays during the second
day's Akihabara visit, and the script counts it as her first of seven)*
The protagonist wanders into the maid cafe alone in Akihabara. Aya explains the
rules, the seating charge, the time limit. She draws a cat on his omurice. She
teaches him the moe moe kyun spell. He joins in, badly, and the room is warmer
than he expected. She says please come back.

**Visit 2: Remembered**
She remembers him. Welcome back, Master! She says it like it is a genuine
surprise, and it might be. She asks what surprised him most about Japan so far,
and listens to the answer. She mentions she taught herself English on her own.
That is why she was chosen to serve foreign customers. She asks it like a
question she has been saving.

**Visit 3: The Tired Smile**
She sits at his table, which breaks the cafe rules, and does not seem to care.
She tells him the cheerfulness is exhausting. Not the job, the performance. She
once wanted to perform on stage, not in a costume. She is not sure the dream is
realistic. The protagonist can encourage her or not. If he does, she blushes
slightly and says it is easy for you to say, but thank you. It helps hearing
that. This is the first time the mask slips.

**Visit 4: Masks and Uniforms**
She asks why he came to Japan. He gives an answer, and she gives one back. She
studied abroad for a semester in Canada and loved the freedom of it. Coming home
reminded her of the pressure to fit into a prescribed role. She says: here, we
wear uniforms, roles, even smiles like masks. Sometimes I wonder if people ever
see the real me. The line lands harder than she means it to.

**Visit 5: The Braver Feeling**
She looks tired. Her voice is slightly hoarse. She still greets him warmly. She
tells him she has been thinking about quitting. Her parents think the job is
frivolous, not respectable. She does not know what she will do yet. But when I
talk with you, I feel a little braver. She says it the way Sim says things:
casually, without weight, and it lands anyway.

**Visit 6: The Audition**
She is brighter. Something has changed. She auditioned for a small performing
role, a local event. Just applying made her feel alive. She thanks the
protagonist for inspiring her courage, whether directly or indirectly. She is
not just a maid anymore. She is a person sharing part of her journey, and the
protagonist realises he has watched her grow one visit at a time.

**Visit 7: Shiori**
She says this may be the last time. She might leave the cafe soon if her other
path works out. She admits: at first I was just the maid with pink hair. But
with you, I could just be myself. Not a character, not a mask. Then she tells him
her real name. Not Aya. Shiori. She thanks him for being part of her story.
Maybe someday, somewhere, we will meet again. After she quits, the cafe is still
open, but no one there treats the protagonist the way she did. The room is the
same. The warmth is absent.

### Hana — Seven Silences

Hana is the confidant who almost does not exist. She shelves books in the reading
circle room, avoids eye contact, and flees the instant anyone tries to talk to
her. Her arc is told through gestures, notes, and silences long before she
speaks. The protagonist finds the reading circle on a free afternoon, on an
errand: exams are coming, he needs somewhere quiet to study and a department
document he cannot find, and Hikari sends him to the library — the place
students go when they actually need to get something done. Some people there
are reading, some are studying, and the circle shares the same quiet rooms.
He takes the corridor she describes. The door is open. Four chairs, a shelf of
returns, and a girl who glances at him once and then looks away.

A side character — one of the circle's own regulars — is reading in the corner.
He explains in one line: she is like a ghost. She is here every week. Nobody
has heard her speak.

**Visit 1: The Ghost**
The protagonist sits down with a book. Hana shelves returns on the other side of
the room without looking at him. When he leaves, she glances at the door. He
does not notice. The side character does.

**Visit 2: The Linger**
She places a book on the shelf nearest the protagonist. She lingers a second
longer than last time. He sees her badge for the first time: Hana. She notices
him noticing and retreats behind the returns shelf. The side character turns a
page and does not comment.

**Visit 3: The Whisper**
She tries to speak. The protagonist is close enough to hear. What comes out is
inaudible, a breath shaped like a word that dies before it arrives. She panics,
grabs a stack of returns, and disappears behind the shelves. The side character
says, without looking up: she does that. Give her time.

**Visit 4: The Art Book**
She carries a book across the room, an oversized art book with Impressionist
Masters on the cover. She gets halfway to the protagonist, freezes, hugs it to
her chest, and retreats. The protagonist may pick up a similar art book from the
shelf and leave it open on the table. When he leaves, his book is closed and hers
is on the table, open to a page she chose.

**Visit 5: The Note**
She sits at the same table, pretending to read, peeking over the top of her
book. She does not run when the protagonist looks. When he leaves, there is a
folded note on the table. Thank you for not giving up on me. The handwriting is
small and careful, the kind of writing that took courage to produce.

**Visit 6: The Confession**
She speaks. Fragments, slowly, each one costing visible effort. No friends.
Self-hatred. Secret art books. Lawyer parents who have already decided her
life. Feeling trapped. She shows him her sketchbook. The drawings are real,
raw, and talented, and the protagonist understands that this is the first time
anyone has seen them. She watches him look at her work the way the chef watched
him eat the first bowl.

**Visit 7: The Charm**
She told her parents the truth. They were angry that she had hidden it, then they
apologised. She is leaving the reading circle to pursue art. She gives the
protagonist a handmade charm: a tiny cloth strap with a brush and palette
stitched in thread. She says: you gave me courage. Without you, I would still
be hiding. Then she leaves, and the reading circle is just a room with chairs
and shelves and quiet. The side character turns a page.

## Hikari Parallel Arc — The Compass Turns

Hikari is not a seven-visit confidant. She is the host sister, the programmer
who leaked into the simulation, and the one person who stays. Her arc runs
parallel to the confidant arcs and is driven by sister_affection. She is not
one of the seven-visit chains: the game tracks her separately, with her own
visit count (hikari_visits) for WIRE progress and sister_affection for the
relationship itself.

### The Run Becomes Recurring

The first river run is already scripted. It becomes a recurring after-school
activity, not a numbered chain. The seven beats the game planned are:

1. **The first run** *(already scripted)* — how badly it goes, and that she
invites him anyway.
2. **The route** — she knows every vending machine, every crack in the path.
She runs it the way the chef makes ramen: from memory, without thinking.
3. **The evening he keeps up** — he matches her pace for ten minutes, and she
is surprised enough to say so. What it costs her is that he sees her face when
she is not performing.
4. **The night neither of them says anything** — they run, they stop, they
drink from the vending machine, and they walk home in silence. It is the most
honest either of them has been.
5. **The rain** — the bridge in the rain, and the first true thing she says
about the house. She says her father closes the amado the same way every night,
and she used to think that was normal, and now she is not sure what normal means.
6. **The night she asks him to run somewhere that is not home** — she takes
him to a part of the river he has never seen, and she says she comes here when
the house feels too small. He asks what makes the house feel small. She does not
answer.
7. **The last run** — she is faster than she has ever been, and he cannot keep
up. She stops at the bridge and looks at the water. She says: do not you ever
feel like we have done this before? He does not know what to say. She does not
expect him to.

### The Unease Grows

Between the runs, Hikari starts noticing things. A face in the crowd she has
seen before, in exactly the same spot. A sentence she has said before, heard
coming back to her from someone else. A memory that does not line up with what
the protagonist remembers. She tests him with small questions: does this street
look right to you? Did I tell you about the bakery before, or after, the first
run? She is not asking because she is curious. She is asking because she is
afraid.

### The Small Truths

As sister_affection rises, she begins to sneak him information. A name he should
not know. A date that does not exist in the calendar. A detail about the house
that could not be true and is. She never calls it a simulation. She says things
like: I wrote that. I do not remember writing it, but I recognise the
handwriting. She says it casually, as if she is describing a habit she cannot
break.

### The Threshold

The final stage of her arc is the fork. She reveals what she is: a fragment of a
programmer who leaked into the code. She offers to guide the protagonist toward
reality, and she expects to be refused, or to be hated for it. Her warmth does
not change when she says it. That is what makes it land.

## The Fortune Teller — Three Cards

The Fortune Teller appears once per chapter, in a location the protagonist is
already visiting. She never seeks him out. He turns a corner and there she is,
with her cards on a folding table and a smile that knows too much.

**Encounter 1: Asakusa**
He finds her on the temple steps, between the incense sellers and the omikuji
stalls. She reads his palm and says: you have been here before. Not this temple.
This moment. He asks what that means. She says come back when you are ready to
hear it. You will not be. You will come back anyway.

**Encounter 2: Harajuku**
She is on a side street off Takeshita-dori, between a vintage shop and a
crepe stand. She flips a card: a figure lying on a table, wires at the wrists.
She says: the cards say you are being looked after. I would ask by whom, if I
were you. He asks. She does not answer. She packs up her table and walks away.

**Encounter 3: The Night Corner**
She is on the street near the house, on a corner the protagonist has passed a
hundred times. She is not reading cards. She is looking at the house. She says:
you are almost at the door. The hard part is not opening it. The hard part is
deciding which side you want to be on. Then she is gone, and the corner is empty,
and the streetlight buzzes the way it always has.

## Tokyo Location Visits

Between confidant visits, the protagonist explores Tokyo. These are not
confidant arcs. They are the city itself, and the cultural shock that comes
with living inside it. Each location carries one or two scenes, a specific
cultural moment, and sometimes a thread of the simulation mystery.

### Asakusa — The Old City

The protagonist visits Asakusa on a free afternoon. The contrast with Akihabara
is immediate: where Akihabara is neon and noise, Asakusa is stone, incense, and
the kind of quiet that sits on a place like weather.

The Kaminarimon gate, the nakamise market lane, the temple beyond it. The
protagonist watches people clap, bow, and ring the bell. He does not know the
order. An old woman shows him: bow, clap twice, bow. He tries it. It feels
like borrowing someone else habit. He does it anyway.

The Fortune Teller is here, on the temple steps, between the incense sellers.
This is her first encounter (see above).

Cultural moment: he buys an omikuji fortune from a machine. It comes out in
Japanese. He cannot read it. A stranger beside him reads it aloud: excellent
fortune. The stranger smiles and walks away. The protagonist keeps the paper
and ties it to the rack, the way everyone else does. He does not know why. It
feels right.

### Odaiba — The Future That Already Happened

Odaiba is the opposite of Asakusa. Artificial island, futuristic architecture, a
giant Gundam statue, and a beach that faces the city across the bay. The
protagonist comes here on a free afternoon and the place feels wrong in a way
he cannot name.

It is too clean. Too planned. The buildings are the right height and the right
colour and the streets curve the way streets in a render curve. He has a moment
where the water looks flat, like a texture that has not loaded properly, and
then it is just water again.

Cultural moment: he eats at a conveyor-belt sushi restaurant. Plates come at
him on a moving belt, colour-coded by price. He grabs what looks good and
stacks the plates. The staff counts the stack at the end. The system is
effortless and he feels like he has been eating this way his whole life, which
is a strange thing to feel after one visit.

Simulation thread: the Gundam statue blinks. Just once, for a frame. The
protagonist blinks too, and when he opens his eyes the statue is the same. He
does not mention it to anyone.

### Harajuku — The Crowd That Becomes A Person

Takeshita-dori on a Sunday afternoon is the densest crowd the protagonist has
ever stood in. He is carried by it more than he walks through it. Crepe stands,
vintage shops, accessory stores the size of closets, and fashion that does not
exist anywhere else in the world.

Cultural moment: he is stopped by a girl handing out flyers for a vintage
clothing shop. She is dressed in full lolita fashion, layers of lace and ribbon,
and she talks to him in English she learned from YouTube. She asks where he is
from. He tells her. She says: cool, I have never left Tokyo. She hands him a
flyer and is gone. The interaction lasts forty seconds and he remembers it for
days.

The Fortune Teller is here too, on a side street (see above).

Simulation thread: the crowd thins for one second. Just one. A gap opens around
the protagonist, large enough to stand in, and the people on either side keep
walking as if nothing happened. Then the crowd closes and it is a normal Sunday
again. Nobody else noticed.

### Yokohama Chinatown — The Other Foreigner

The protagonist takes a day trip to Yokohama. Chinatown is a ten-minute walk
from the station, a wall of red gates and restaurant signs. He walks through
it and feels something he has not felt since the airport: the particular
displacement of being a foreigner in a country that is not your own, standing
in a district that is foreign to the country it sits inside.

Cultural moment: he eats nikuman from a street stall, and the vendor speaks to
him in Chinese, then Japanese, then gestures. The protagonist points and nods
and the transaction works. He realises this is the third language barrier he
has crossed this month, and that the barrier is thinner than he thought.

Simulation thread: he sees a restaurant sign that says, in English, WELCOME
HOME. The words sit strangely. He has never been to Yokohama before. He passes
the sign and does not look back, but the words stay.

## Cultural Shock Moments

These are small, self-contained scenes that happen between the larger arcs. They
are the texture of living in a country you do not fully understand.

**The Onsen** — the protagonist goes to a public bath for the first time. The
moment of undressing in a room full of strangers is a wall he has to walk
through. On the other side of it, the water is hot enough to make his shoulders
drop, and the tiled room is quiet in a way that has nothing to do with volume.
He sits in the water and the water does not care where he is from.

**The Convenience Store at 2 AM** — he cannot sleep. He walks to the FamilyMart
on the corner in his slippers. The lights are too bright. The rice balls are
arranged with the precision of a museum. The cashier greets him in Japanese and
he answers in Japanese and the transaction is two words and a bow and it works.
He eats the rice ball on the walk home and the night air is cool on his face.

**The Karaoke Box** — Hikari takes him to a karaoke booth. A small room, a
screen, two microphones. She sings without hesitation. He does not know any
of the songs. She finds an English one and hands him the microphone. He sings
badly. She cheers. The room is too small for the noise they are making and
neither of them cares.

**The Train at Rush Hour** — he takes the Chuo line at 8:15 AM. The doors
open and the platform pushes him in. He does not choose where to stand. The
train chooses. He is pressed against a salaryman who is reading a paperback
with one hand and holding the rail with the other and whose expression does
not change for the entire twenty-minute ride. When the protagonist gets off, he
is standing exactly where the doors will open, without having moved.

**The Festival** — a matsuri in the neighbourhood. Lanterns, food stalls, a
portable shrine carried by men in happi coats. The protagonist eats yakitori
and watches the shrine pass. Tetsuo is there, in a happi coat, carrying a rope.
He does not look at the protagonist. The protagonist did not know Tetsuo did
this. Miharu is beside him, watching, and she sees the protagonist and waves.
The festival smells like charcoal and summer.

## Act 3: The Glitch

Act 3 is where the leaks stop being atmospheric and start being structural.
The world is not just glitching. It is breaking down. This is the act where
broken things start appearing on purpose — repeated sentences, blurred faces,
the dream bleeding into waking life — because the test is entering its hardest
phase and the simulation is straining to hold the subject inside it.

### Stage 1: The World Repeats

Tetsuo says the same sentence he said last Tuesday. Word for word, at the same
point in the same meal. The protagonist notices because he has heard it before,
not because the delivery changed. Miharu pours tea and her hand stops mid-pour
for half a second, as if she forgot what she was doing, and then continues. The
professor writes a date on the board that is three months in the future, looks at
it, erases it, and writes the correct one without comment.

### Stage 2: The Faces Blur

The protagonist starts noticing the same face in different places. A woman on
the train who was also at the university gate. A man at the ramen shop who was
also at the convenience store. He is not sure if this is Tokyo being a city, or
something else. He does not mention it. Then Hikari mentions it first. She says:
do you ever see someone and think you have seen them before, in exactly the same
place, doing exactly the same thing? She says it like a question about the
weather. It is not a question about the weather.

### Stage 3: The Dream Breaks Through

The dream sequences get longer and more specific. The hospital room is no longer
a flash of white light. It has walls, a bed, wires. The voices have genders. One
is patient, the other is clinical, and they discuss the protagonist by number,
not name. Subject 04. Consolidation at 78 percent. He is starting to talk in his
sleep, which is new, and the clinical voice says that is not supposed to happen
yet.

### Stage 4: Tetsuo Breaks

If the protagonist has deviated from the expected path, pursued truth, pushed
against the routine, Tetsuo is the first non-Hikari character to visibly glitch.
A repeated sentence, delivered twice in a row with the same intonation. A pause
that is one beat too long. An answer to a question nobody asked. He does not
seem aware of it. Miharu does, and she covers for him the way a wife covers for a
husband who has forgotten a name, which makes it worse.

### Stage 5: Hikari Breaks Through

Hikari asks a question she has already asked. She hears herself do it. She
stops. She looks at the protagonist and her face changes, not into fear but into
recognition, the expression of a person who has just found the word they have
been reaching for. She says: I wrote that. I do not remember writing it, but I
recognise the handwriting. She is not talking about a sentence. She is talking
about the house, the street, the amado, the river, the university. She is
talking about the whole world.

---

## Act 4: The End

Act 4 is short. It is the moment the coat comes off and the thriller underneath
is visible. Everything the protagonist has built, everyone he has gotten close
to, all of it is measured against a single question: is any of it real?

### The Revelation

Hikari takes the protagonist to the river one last time. Not to run. To talk.

She tells him what she is. Not a student, not a sister, not a girl who runs to
forget. A fragment of a programmer who was building the simulation and leaked
into it while the code was still wet. She remembers writing the river path. She
remembers choosing the name Hikari because it meant light and she thought that
was funny. She does not remember being inside the simulation until the first
dream, and she is not sure that memory is real either.

She tells him what he is. Subject 04. A test subject in a government-funded
facility, in a medicine-induced coma, inside a simulation designed to test
whether a foreigner can successfully assimilate into a future Japanese society
facing a population crisis. The university, the host family, the confidants,
the river, the ramen, the photographs: all of it is the test. The attributes
are the score.

She tells him what the departures were. The chef, Sim, Orson, Aya, Hana. They
left because the simulation removes a confidant when their arc completes, to test
whether the subject can maintain emotional equilibrium after loss. Getting
close to someone is what makes them leave. That is not the theme of the game.
It is the protocol.

She does not tell him what to do. She offers to guide him toward reality, and
she expects to be refused, or to be hated for it. Her warmth does not change
when she says it. That is what makes it land.

### The Three Endings

**The Escape**

The protagonist accepts. Hikari walks him through what she knows of the facility
layout, which is incomplete, because she is a fragment and fragments do not have
blueprints. But she knows the direction of the door, and she knows the code for
the pod lock, because she wrote the lock and she wrote the code.

The escape is not a boss fight. It is a walk. The simulation peels away in
layers: the river goes first, then the university, then the house, then the
street, then the sky. Each layer is a goodbye to someone who is not there
anymore. The last thing to go is Hikari voice, telling him which way the door is.

There is a second version of this ending, and it is earned. The confidants were
never just characters — they were constructs with a directive: help the subject
if he asks for the way out. A confidant the protagonist maxed out (their full
run) remembers what they were built for. If he treated them like people, they
remember it too, and one of them can be there at the door — a voice on the
other side, a hand on the lock, a final goodbye that is also a thank-you. An
under-massed escape still works; it is just lonelier, and the layers peel away
with no voices in them at all.

He wakes up in a room he has never seen. White walls, a bed, wires at the
wrists. A woman is sitting beside the bed. She looks like Hikari, but older, and
tired, and real. She says: welcome back. It took longer than I thought it would.

The screen goes black.

**The Blue Pill**

The protagonist refuses. He chooses to stay. Hikari does not argue. She nods,
and she does not look surprised, and she does not look disappointed, and that is
worse than either.

The simulation continues. The river runs. The university lectures. The house
eats at eight. The protagonist keeps visiting the places that are left, but the
people who made them matter are gone. The ramen shop is a shuttered door. The
club room is just a room. Lupin's Den is a coffee shop. The maid cafe is warm and
full and no one there knows his name. The reading circle has a new volunteer
who does not look up.

He runs the river alone. He stops at the bridge. The water goes one way and
comes back the same way, and it never asks him anything.

The screen holds.

**The Failure**

The protagonist never reaches the threshold — or reaches it and is not
assimilated enough to matter. The simulation detects that the subject has not
formed sufficient emotional bonds to proceed, and the test ends on its own
terms.

What follows is not an ending so much as a wind-down. The people who were
trying to keep him inside stop pretending. Moving around gets harder — the
city narrows, the routes loop, the faces go flat. He keeps going anyway,
because stopping is the only thing left that is his. Eventually the world
itself gives up on him.

The last thing the protagonist sees is the house. The lights are on. The family
is at the table. Miharu has poured the tea. Tetsuo is saying the thing he always
says. The amado closes the way it always closes.

Then it cuts to white — and for one moment the simulation's own voice comes
through, clinical and bored: *this one was also a failed experiment.* A pause.
*Bring in the next participant.*

The screen goes white, and then the title screen.

---

## Epilogue

However the story ends, there is one last coda: the characters' thoughts, played
one final time. Not a scene — a montage of the people he met, each given a
single line from inside their own head. The chef tasting the broth one more
time. Sim raising a camera that is not there. Orson polishing a glass in an
empty bar. Aya mid-spin, laughing at no one. Hana's pencil moving across a
sketchbook. Hikari at the river, watching the water go one way and come back
the same way.

It is the game's thesis stated without dialogue: the simulation can remove a
person. It cannot remove the memory of a person. Whether he escaped, stayed, or
was discarded, they were real while he was there — and that is the only verdict
that matters.

---

## Afterword

The game thesis, stated in the first lecture and proved in the last run, is
that a country getting smaller has to decide what it does about that. The
simulation is the answer the fictional government chose: test whether outsiders
can become insiders, measure the cost, and decide whether the cost is worth
paying.

The protagonist cost was everyone he got close to. The simulation removed them,
one by one, to see if he would break. He did not break. He grieved. There is a
difference, and the game knows it, and the simulation does not.

That is the thing Hikari wrote into the code without meaning to. The simulation
can remove a person. It cannot remove the memory of a person. The omurice cat,
the wooden spoon, the camera strap, the wolf badge, the cloth charm: these are
not inventory items. They are the proof that the people who gave them were real,
even if the world they were given in was not.

The ending the player chooses is not about whether the simulation was real. It
is about whether the connections inside it were. Hikari is the one who says it,
and she is the only person who can, because she is the only person who knows both
sides:

It was not real. You were. That is enough.
